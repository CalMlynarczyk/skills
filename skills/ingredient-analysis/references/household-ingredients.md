## How to read the label itself

**Disclosure is much weaker than for food.** In the US there's no general federal requirement to list every ingredient in a cleaning product. California and New York disclosure laws have changed this substantially in practice, since manufacturers tend to standardize nationally, but "inert" or "other ingredients" can still cover a lot. Say when the list is likely incomplete.

**Concentration is often the missing variable.** Disinfectants must state active ingredient percentages; general cleaners frequently don't. Without concentration, most risk questions can only be answered directionally.

**Signal words are regulated and informative.** DANGER indicates corrosive or highly toxic; WARNING is moderate; CAUTION is mild. These reflect acute hazard testing and are more reliable than most of what's on the front of the bottle.

**EPA registration number** means antimicrobial efficacy claims were reviewed. The label's **contact time** — how long the surface must stay visibly wet — is a tested number, and most people wipe well before it. If someone is asking whether a disinfectant works, that's usually the real answer.

**Dilution ratio** transforms the analysis. A concentrate at 1:64 delivers a very different exposure than a ready-to-use spray with the same ingredient list.

## Functional roles

**Surfactants** — the workhorses. They reduce surface tension and lift soils.
- *Anionic* (sodium lauryl sulfate, sodium laureth sulfate, linear alkylbenzene sulfonate) — strong cleaning, high foam, more irritating
- *Nonionic* (alcohol ethoxylates, alkyl polyglucosides, cocamide DEA) — good on grease, low foam, gentler
- *Cationic* (quaternary ammonium compounds) — antimicrobial and antistatic; the active in most fabric softeners and many disinfectants. Cationics and anionics neutralize each other, which is why detergent and softener go in separate dispensers.
- *Amphoteric* (cocamidopropyl betaine) — mild, common in baby and "gentle" formulations

**Chelators / builders** — sequester calcium and magnesium so surfactants aren't deactivated by hard water. Tetrasodium EDTA, sodium citrate, sodium gluconate, phosphonates, and formerly phosphates (largely removed from laundry and dish detergent over eutrophication concerns).

**Solvents** — dissolve what water can't. Glycol ethers (2-butoxyethanol, propylene glycol ethers), isopropyl alcohol, ethanol, d-limonene, terpenes. The main inhalation exposure in most cleaning products comes from here.

**Alkalis and acids** — sodium hydroxide and sodium carbonate for grease and drains; citric, lactic, phosphoric, sulfamic, and hydrochloric acid for scale, rust, and toilet bowls. Both ends of the pH scale are where the corrosive injuries come from.

**Oxidizers / bleaches** — sodium hypochlorite (chlorine bleach), hydrogen peroxide, sodium percarbonate ("oxygen bleach"), peracetic acid.

**Enzymes** — protease, amylase, lipase, mannanase in laundry and dish detergent. Targeted soil breakdown at low temperature. Notable as a respiratory sensitizer in manufacturing settings; encapsulation in consumer products makes this a minor concern.

**Antimicrobial actives** — quats (benzalkonium chloride, didecyldimethylammonium chloride), sodium hypochlorite, hydrogen peroxide, alcohols, phenolics, thymol, citric acid, peracetic acid.

**Preservatives** — the water phase of any product needs them. Isothiazolinones (MIT, CMIT, BIT), formaldehyde releasers (DMDM hydantoin, quaternium-15, imidazolidinyl urea), phenoxyethanol, benzisothiazolinone.

**Fragrance, dye, opacifiers, pH adjusters, thickeners** — sensory and stability. Fragrance is the single largest disclosure gap in the category and a leading cause of contact dermatitis.

## Acute hazards — check these first

These cause far more documented injury than any chronic-exposure ingredient debate, and they're frequently absent from ingredient commentary. When the product involves any of them, lead with it.

**Chemical incompatibilities:**
- **Bleach + ammonia** (including many glass cleaners and some urine-soiled surfaces) → chloramine gas. A common cause of household chemical exposure calls.
- **Bleach + acid** (toilet bowl cleaner, vinegar, some rust removers) → chlorine gas.
- **Bleach + hydrogen peroxide** → exothermic, oxygen release.
- **Different drain cleaners mixed** → violent reaction, potential eruption of caustic material.
- **Vinegar + hydrogen peroxide combined in one container** → peracetic acid, an irritant. Sequentially on a surface is fine; premixed and stored is not.

**Corrosive injury** — drain openers, oven cleaners, and toilet bowl cleaners cause the most severe chemical burns and eye injuries in the home. Concentrated caustics are more dangerous than most of what people worry about.

**Child ingestion** — laundry and dishwasher pods produce a disproportionate share of serious pediatric exposures, because the membrane dissolves rapidly and the concentrated contents are caustic and can be aspirated. Concentrated any-product stored under the sink is a comparable concern.

**Ventilation** — aerosol and spray products in a small closed bathroom is the realistic worst-case inhalation scenario in a home.

**Never decant into food or beverage containers.** Uncommon in ingredient discussions, common in poison center data.

## Contested ingredients, handled honestly

**Quaternary ammonium compounds (quats)** — effective antimicrobials. The occupational evidence linking cleaning-product exposure to asthma and new-onset reactive airway disease in professional cleaners is reasonably strong (Tier B, arguably A for the occupational population); extrapolation to household use with normal ventilation is much weaker. Also a driver of antimicrobial resistance selection, which is a population-level concern rather than an individual one. Reasonable summary: relevant if you have asthma, use disinfectants heavily, or clean professionally; minor for occasional household use with ventilation.

**Fragrance** — the strongest legitimate flag in this category, and mostly not for the reasons it's usually raised. The real, well-established issue is contact allergy and sensitization; the EU's list of labeled fragrance allergens is a useful reference. The secondary issue is that terpenes (limonene, pinene) react with ambient ozone to form formaldehyde and ultrafine particles indoors — a real measured phenomenon whose health significance at household concentrations is unresolved. Phthalates as fragrance fixatives are less common than they were but not gone, and are not disclosed.

**Phthalates** — endocrine activity is well established in animal models, and human epidemiology on reproductive endpoints is suggestive but confounded. Regulatory restriction has moved steadily, particularly for children's products. Main exposure routes are food contact materials and PVC rather than cleaners. When flagged, note the route.

**Triclosan** — banned by FDA from consumer antiseptic washes in 2016 for lack of demonstrated benefit over plain soap, plus resistance concerns. Largely gone from consumer products; if it appears, note the product is probably old.

**1,4-dioxane** — not an intentional ingredient. A byproduct of the ethoxylation used to make ethoxylated surfactants (anything with "-eth-" in the name, like sodium laureth sulfate). Manufacturers strip it, but residuals persist and several states now limit them. Worth mentioning only when ethoxylated surfactants are prominent and the person is asking about trace contaminants.

**Formaldehyde releasers** — release small amounts of formaldehyde over time to preserve the product. Formaldehyde is a Group 1 carcinogen by inhalation at occupational concentrations; the relevant consumer question is sensitization and contact dermatitis, not cancer, and that distinction should be stated rather than blurred.

**"Green" and "non-toxic" claims** — largely unregulated as marketing terms. Third-party certifications (EPA Safer Choice, Green Seal, EcoLogo) have actual criteria and are worth more than the front-of-bottle language. Note also that plant-derived doesn't mean gentle: d-limonene and pine oil are effective solvents and effective irritants.

**Essential-oil-based cleaners** — thymol and citric acid have genuine EPA-registered antimicrobial efficacy. Concentrated essential oils are also skin sensitizers and, for cats, a real toxicity risk.

## Personal care specifics

Leave-on products warrant more attention than rinse-off, because exposure duration and skin contact area are much larger.

- **Sensitization risk** is the dominant realistic adverse outcome: fragrance allergens, MIT/CMIT, formaldehyde releasers, and preservative systems generally.
- **Sunscreen actives** — oxybenzone and octinoxate show measurable systemic absorption in FDA studies, which established that absorption occurs but not that it causes harm; several jurisdictions have restricted them for coral reef reasons rather than human health. Mineral filters (zinc oxide, titanium dioxide) are the alternative. The one Tier A conclusion here is that using sunscreen beats not using it.
- **Parabens** — weak estrogenic activity in vitro, orders of magnitude below endogenous estradiol. Human outcome evidence is thin. They're effective preservatives, and the reformulation away from them drove the rise in isothiazolinone use, which produced a measurable increase in contact dermatitis. A clean example of a substitution that may have made things worse.
- **Talc** — the concern is asbestos contamination from shared geological deposits, not talc itself. Asbestos-free certification is the relevant question.
- **Retinoids** — contraindicated in pregnancy; genuine, not precautionary.

## Pets

Raise these when a pet is in the picture and the product is relevant.

- **Cats** metabolize phenols and many essential oils poorly. Pine oil, tea tree oil, citrus oils, and phenolic disinfectants are meaningfully risky, particularly in enclosed spaces or on surfaces cats walk on and then groom.
- **Benzalkonium chloride** on floors can cause oral ulceration in cats that walk through it before it dries.
- **Ethylene glycol** (antifreeze, some winter products) is lethal in small amounts and tastes sweet.
- **Birds** are exceptionally sensitive to aerosols and to PTFE fumes from overheated non-stick cookware.
- **Xylitol** in foods and some personal care products is severely toxic to dogs.
- **General rule**: let treated surfaces dry fully and ventilate before letting animals back in.
