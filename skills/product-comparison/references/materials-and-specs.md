# Materials and Specs

For the feature-driven half of a comparison: devices, appliances, tools, furniture, cookware, textiles, and home goods. Read this when the decision turns on what a product is built from and what it can do, rather than on an ingredient list.

## 1. Reading a spec sheet

A manufacturer spec sheet is accurate about what it measures and silent about everything else. The useful skill is noticing the omission.

**Every performance number has test conditions.** Battery life, range, throughput, noise level, capacity, and efficiency are all measured under a protocol, and the protocol is chosen to flatter. Ask what conditions produced the number: what load, what temperature, what duty cycle, what state of charge. When two products quote the same metric under different protocols, the numbers aren't comparable and the table should say so rather than lining them up as if they were.

**Peak versus sustained.** A number that describes a burst — peak power, maximum speed, boost clock, initial suction — tells you little about the experience of using the thing continuously. Sustained figures are harder to find and much more predictive. When only the peak is published, that's a signal in itself.

**The units carry assumptions.** Watts of input power say nothing about output. Thread count is inflated by counting plies. Megapixels are decoupled from image quality. Peak horsepower on a shop tool is measured at conditions the circuit can't supply. When a spec is easy to inflate, it stops being a comparison axis and starts being marketing.

**What's missing is a spec.** No published weight usually means it's heavy. No noise rating usually means it's loud. No stated repair policy usually means there isn't one. Absent numbers cluster around weaknesses, and a comparison that only tabulates published figures systematically favors whoever discloses least.

**Third-party testing outranks manufacturer claims**, and independent teardowns outrank both for anything about construction. Weight this in the table by labeling the source of each number.

## 2. Materials as durability predictors

Material choice is where cost-cutting shows up first, and it predicts service life better than most performance specs.

**Metals.** Stainless grades differ meaningfully (304 for corrosion resistance, 430 for magnetic cheapness); cast versus stamped changes rigidity and thermal behavior; aluminum is light and conducts well but deforms and anodizing wears through. "Stainless steel" on a spec sheet without a grade is a hint that the grade isn't a selling point. Watch for structural components in cast zinc or pot metal, which is fine under compression and fails at threads and hinges.

**Plastics.** Polycarbonate and ABS take impact; polypropylene resists chemicals and fatigue and is what living hinges are made of; polystyrene is brittle and cheap. Glass-filled nylon in a gear or bracket is a genuine engineering choice; unfilled nylon in the same place isn't. In anything that holds load or heat, an unspecified "durable plastic" is worth a question.

**Coatings.** PTFE, ceramic sol-gel, seasoned iron, hard anodizing, and powder coat all have different wear mechanisms and lifespans. Coatings are consumable; the question isn't whether it will wear but how long and what's underneath when it does. A coated surface over a good substrate is repairable; over a bad one it's disposable.

**Wood and boards.** Solid hardwood, veneered plywood, MDF, and particleboard sit on a steep quality gradient, mostly around moisture behavior and whether fasteners hold on the second assembly. Furniture that will be moved or that lives near water changes this from a preference to a constraint.

**Textiles and foam.** Fiber content, weave, and finish beat brand claims. For foam, density (lb/ft³) predicts how long it resists compression set — this is the number mattress and cushion marketing most avoids publishing.

**Batteries.** Chemistry (LFP versus NMC versus lead-acid), rated cycle life, and whether the pack is user-replaceable determine the real lifespan of anything cordless. A sealed battery in a five-year product is a disposal date.

## 3. Construction and repairability tells

These predict how a product ages, and they're often visible in photos, manuals, or teardowns even when specs are thin.

- **Fasteners versus adhesive and clips.** Screws mean serviceable; glue and heat-staked plastic mean the first failure is terminal.
- **Wear parts availability.** Gaskets, filters, belts, blades, hinges, batteries. A published parts diagram with orderable part numbers is one of the strongest longevity signals available.
- **Modularity.** Whether a common failure (a frayed cord, a dead pump, a stripped hinge) takes out the whole unit.
- **Consumable lock-in.** Proprietary refills, cartridges, or pods move cost from purchase to ownership and remove the option to shop later. This deserves a table row of its own whenever it applies.
- **Firmware and account dependency.** For connected products: does it work offline, does it require an account, does the manufacturer commit to a support window, can it be un-bricked. A cloud dependency converts a purchase into a subscription risk.
- **Warranty as a signal.** The length and — more informatively — what's excluded. A warranty that covers the motor for ten years and everything else for one is telling you where the failures are.

## 4. Certifications and standards

Some marks carry test-backed information, some are self-declared, some are trade groups. Distinguishing them prevents both false confidence and unearned skepticism.

**Generally test-backed:** UL / ETL / CSA listing (electrical safety, tested to a named standard), NSF (materials and sanitation for food and water contact), Energy Star (measured efficiency under a protocol), IP ratings (specific ingress tests — IP67 and IPX4 mean concrete different things), ANSI/ISO/ASTM references to a numbered standard, EPA registration for antimicrobial claims, WaterSense, DOE efficiency ratings.

**Weaker or self-declared:** "medical grade", "professional grade", "commercial quality", "hospital strength", "aerospace aluminum", "military spec" without a MIL-STD number. These are adjectives.

**Read what the mark covers.** A certification usually applies to one property under one test, not to overall quality. A CE mark is a self-declaration of conformity, not a safety test result. An IP rating covers the tested ingress condition, not immersion in general. When a product cites a standard by number, that number is worth a search; when it cites a standard by vibe, note the difference.

## 5. Cost of ownership

The purchase price is one term. Normalize to the unit that reflects the decision so the table can be read across.

- **Consumables** — filters, pods, blades, bags, refills. Multiply by realistic replacement frequency, not the manufacturer's optimistic interval.
- **Energy and water** — meaningful for anything that runs daily. Efficiency labels usually publish an annual estimate that's directly comparable.
- **Expected service life** — from warranty, materials, and repairability. Price per year of service is often the number that overturns an intuition about which product is expensive.
- **Switching and lock-in costs** — accessories that carry over, ecosystem dependencies, whether the incumbent's consumables are stranded.

Show both the raw price and the normalized figure. One clears the budget; the other decides the comparison.

## 6. Where the real differences usually are

Comparisons drift toward whatever is easiest to tabulate. The attributes that actually determine whether someone is glad they bought it tend to be these, and they're worth deliberately checking:

- **Fit and physical constraint** — does it fit the counter, the doorway, the cabinet, the existing mount, the outlet. A disqualifier that spec tables rarely surface.
- **Maintenance friction** — is it dishwasher safe, how many parts come apart to clean it, does the filter need a tool. This drives whether a product gets used.
- **Noise** — routinely undisclosed, routinely the reason a product ends up in a closet.
- **Failure mode** — does it degrade gracefully or fail all at once, and does failing take out anything else.
- **What people report after a year**, rather than at unboxing. Long-term reviews and warranty complaints surface the failure that specs never predict.
